# Army Aviation magazine Afghanistan source record — 2010–2012

## Status and authority

This source record consolidates Afghanistan-relevant findings from the `ARMY AVIATION` magazine issues currently available to the project.

It supplements:

- `docs/50-afghanistan-force-basing-aviation-2010-2011.md`;
- `docs/52-army-aviation-vignettes-and-coin-intelligence-metrics.md`;
- basis-specific historical and AirOps source records.

It is historical research evidence only. It does **not** change the active OMW air ORBAT, local inventories, player slots, AI SQUADRONs, statics, warehouse stocks, Mission Editor placement, or DCS/MOOSE acceptance.

The active campaign ORBAT remains governed by `docs/19-active-air-orbat-decisions.md` and the applicable basis-specific manifests.

## Source handling

The magazine identifies itself as the official journal of the Army Aviation Association of America (AAAA), but its publication disclaimer states that views expressed are those of the individual authors and do not necessarily reflect the official position of the Department of Defense, the U.S. Army, AAAA, or the publisher.

Accordingly, the magazine is not treated wholesale as `DIRECT_OFFICIAL`.

OMW uses the existing evidence classes from document 50:

- `SECONDARY` for professional articles and award narratives;
- `SOURCE_DERIVED` where a clearly identified Army/PAO image caption or other first-party material is reproduced but the magazine remains the immediate source;
- `LEAD_ONLY` for indirect, political, promotional, or insufficiently sourced quantitative claims;
- `SOURCE_CONFLICT` when the magazine contains internally inconsistent location or command descriptions.

Publication date alone does not determine relevance. A 2012 issue may contain an explicit retrospective account of a 2011 operation and is then `IN_PERIOD` for that event. Likewise, an early-2010 issue may be retained as `PRE_PERIOD_CONTINUITY` where it documents a capability, unit, or operating pattern that immediately precedes the OMW scenario period.

# Findings by issue

## `avn_10_02.pdf` — February 2010

### Apache deployed-aircraft OPTEMPO

The AH-64D update states that deployed Apache aircraft supporting OEF/OIF were averaging approximately 70–90 flight hours per month, described as roughly five to six times peacetime utilization. It also gives aggregate deployed-fleet figures of more than 10,000 flight hours per month and more than 150,000 combat hours over the preceding year.

Classification:

- time: `PRE_PERIOD_CONTINUITY`;
- evidence: `SECONDARY`;
- use: generalized deployed-Apache utilization and sustainment context only.

Limits:

- the rate is not stated as Afghanistan-only;
- it is not a Kandahar-, Shindand-, Jalalabad-, or unit-specific rate;
- it must not be multiplied by an OMW inventory as if it were a verified local 2010/2011 sortie model without an explicit planning decision.

The same article carries an Army-credited caption showing Company B, 1st Battalion, 82nd Aviation Regiment `Wolfpack` personnel conducting AH-64 preflight activity before mission launch from Kandahar Airfield, Afghanistan.

Classification for the location/type observation: `SOURCE_DERIVED`.

## `avn_10_10.pdf` — October 2010

### Afghanistan rotary-wing environment and survivability

The article `Aircraft Survivability Equipment: Going Beyond the Aircraft Survivability & EW/DCGS - Enterprise` describes Army rotary-wing aviation as especially critical in Afghanistan because of weak road infrastructure, harsh terrain, and large operating areas. It identifies several operational effects directly relevant to OMW mission design:

- valleys channel aircraft movement;
- FOBs become convergence points;
- terrain elevation creates aircraft ceiling constraints;
- line-of-sight terrain creates communications constraints;
- rugged terrain complicates situational awareness;
- predictable rotary-wing patterns can be observed and exploited;
- small-arms and RPG fire are relevant threats to aircraft.

Classification:

- time: `IN_PERIOD`;
- evidence: `SECONDARY`;
- use: environmental, threat, route-pattern, and survivability design context.

This does not establish DCS AI behavior, a universal threat density, or a probability of aircraft loss.

### Shadow 200 increased-endurance operational lead

The issue reports that Company B, Task Force Currahee, 101st Airborne Division launched an experimental Shadow 200 Increased Endurance aircraft on its first official Task Force Currahee mission over Paktika Province on 8 September 2010. The item describes increased endurance, a laser designator, and communications-relay capability.

Classification: `SECONDARY`, `IN_PERIOD`.

Use: UAS/communications/ISR capability context only. It does not change the active OMW UAS ORBAT.

### 4th CAB and MEDEVAC continuity

The issue reports:

- 4th Combat Aviation Brigade soldiers receiving combat patches at Camp Marmal on 11 September 2010;
- Company C, 3rd Battalion, 10th Combat Aviation Brigade casing colors on 5 August 2010 for a fall-2010 OEF deployment;
- a Company B, Task Force Shadow UH-60 crew chief receiving a Purple Heart for wounds received while deployed in OEF at Kandahar Airfield.

Classification: `SECONDARY` / `SOURCE_DERIVED` depending on the credited item; `IN_PERIOD`.

The same professional article states that average deployed sustainment required to meet OPTEMPO can consume about 20 percent of the fleet and recommends planning to induct at least one third of the fleet into RESET during redeployment, potentially up to half shortly after return.

Classification: `SECONDARY`.

Limit: the 20 percent statement is professional planning guidance, not a measured Afghanistan-wide availability factor and not an automatic OMW readiness percentage.

## `avn_11_02.pdf` — February 2011

### Split-based, mixed task-force operations

CW5 Michael L. Reese reports a recent visit to the 4th, 10th, and 101st Combat Aviation Brigades in Afghanistan. The article describes split-based operations in task-force formation as the deployment norm. It states that aviation brigades were divided into multiple task forces operating across multiple regional commands and under varying operational/tactical control arrangements.

The task forces are described as being organized in different combinations to meet mission requirements and as potentially including attack, utility, cargo, observation, fixed-wing, and unmanned aircraft. The article also identifies task-force standardization pilot, maintenance test pilot, safety, and tactical-operations functions.

Classification:

- time: `IN_PERIOD`;
- evidence: `SECONDARY`;
- use: strong professional corroboration for OMW's distributed, mixed-task-force historical model.

Binding interpretation:

```text
CAB identity != one physical ramp
Task-force membership != permanent basing of every aircraft
split-based operation == historically supported employment pattern
mixed task force != license to invent local aircraft counts
```

### Afghan Mi-17 sustainment and airworthiness context

The issue's non-standard rotary-wing discussion states that Mi-17 aircraft had been donated to the Islamic Republic of Afghanistan and were undergoing a substantial return-to-service effort, with fleet management spanning maintenance, supply support, readiness, training, airworthiness, configuration control, and contract support.

An accompanying caption shows Afghan Air Force personnel using a helicopter during flood evacuation in Laghman Province.

Classification: `SECONDARY` / `SOURCE_DERIVED`, `IN_PERIOD`.

Use: Afghan rotary-wing sustainment/training ecosystem context only; no exact AAF inventory or Shindand allocation is derived from it.

## `avn_11_08_09.pdf` — August/September 2011

### 277th Aviation Support Battalion / 10th CAB distributed support network

`Supporting More than the Aviation Brigade in OEF`, by LTC Albert H. Stiller and MAJ William B. Taylor, describes the current 277th Aviation Support Battalion mission supporting the 10th Combat Aviation Brigade in RC-East.

The article states that the ASB:

- was organized into four companies;
- was distributed across 11 FOBs and COPs in RC-East;
- had more than 700 soldiers;
- had most of its personnel at Bagram while every company also maintained personnel at outlying sites.

The Headquarters and Support Company maintenance platoon is described as roughly 89 soldiers supporting vehicles, weapons, ground-support equipment, refuel nozzles, fuel pumps, material-handling equipment, generators, air conditioning, communications/electronics, NVGs, and welding work. Maintenance contact teams were dispatched throughout the area of operations, including the two FARPs operated by the ASB and outlying weapons-support sites.

Classification: `SECONDARY`, `IN_PERIOD`.

### Fuel, FARP, parts, cargo, and DART functions

The article further describes:

- two FARPs operated by the fuel and water platoon in RC-East;
- a 24-hour defuel standby team for the downed-aircraft recovery team;
- petroleum-quality-analysis capability;
- a two-point jump-FARP capability;
- more than 900 Class IX requests and 250 turn-ins per week processed by the SSA at Bagram;
- the Bagram rotary-wing A/DACG averaging about 500 passengers and 160,000 lb of cargo per week;
- a six-MRAP brigade convoy capability for DART, vehicle recovery, and emergency resupply;
- an Aviation Support Company of more than 260 soldiers with approximately 50 contractors;
- internal and contractor phase-maintenance teams for CH-47/UH-60 support;
- armament, avionics, component repair, traveling maintenance teams, FARP armament support, and a robust DART capability.

Classification: `SECONDARY`, `IN_PERIOD`.

OMW use:

- strong historical support for a distributed aviation sustainment network;
- strong support for separating main-hub maintenance from forward contact teams and FARPs;
- useful throughput plausibility for Bagram rotary-wing passenger/cargo handling;
- not an automatic CampaignState stock quantity or warehouse replenishment rate.

### Directory limitation

The Blue Book directory identifies Task Force ODIN-Afghanistan at Bagram but also marks the entry as `Update Not Provided This Year`. The directory line is therefore not used as a fresh 2011 strength or composition source.

## `avn_12_02.pdf` — February 2012

### Task Force Attack dynamic retasking — RC-South, May 2011

`TF Attack Brings Critical Capability to ISAF SOF COIN Efforts`, by LTC Roderick M. Hynes, explicitly describes Regional Command-South, Afghanistan, in May 2011.

The vignette describes a 3rd Battalion, 101st Aviation Regiment / Task Force Attack helicopter assault force conducting a vehicle-interdiction support mission for ISAF SOF and being dynamically retasked by the task-force TOC to a hasty air assault against a newly identified HME site.

The article describes:

- over-the-horizon communications delivering the retask;
- proposed landing zones passed to and refined by crews in flight;
- an aerial weapons team setting conditions;
- coordination between the ground-force commander and air-assault task-force commander;
- final aviation task-force commander approval;
- arrival of the package at the target within about 20 minutes;
- UH-60M air-assault operations in Uruzgan Province in the accompanying Army-credited imagery.

Classification:

- publication: post-period;
- event: `IN_PERIOD`;
- evidence: `SECONDARY` with `SOURCE_DERIVED` imagery;
- use: historical support for time-sensitive retasking, hasty air assault, SOF support, in-flight LZ refinement, and AWT coordination.

Limit: this is a human-command-and-control employment example. It does not establish that DCS AI or MOOSE can reproduce the behavior without separate API/implementation verification.

# Source conflicts and cautions

## 6-6 CAV regional-command inconsistency

The March/April 2012 issue contains two descriptions that cannot both be accepted literally without qualification:

- the Task Force Six Shooters unit-of-the-year narrative places the majority of 2011 operations in the Northern Konar Valley/N2KL, consistent with RC-East;
- a separate Parker Award paragraph states that 6-6 CAV executed the highest operational tempo in `Regional Command-South`.

This is recorded as `SOURCE_CONFLICT` and is treated as a likely editorial or award-summary inconsistency until independently resolved. OMW does not silently change `RC-South` to `RC-East` in the source record.

## Award narratives are not inventory tables

Unit-award entries are useful for aggregate mission volume, maintenance, fuel-handling, and activity patterns, but they are written as recognition narratives. They must not be converted directly into exact local inventories, readiness rates, or warehouse stocks unless the underlying denominator and location are explicit.

## No Shindand inventory derivation from these magazines

The reviewed magazine findings do not supersede the stronger Shindand evidence already held by OMW:

- the July 2011 Afghanistan ORBAT identifies Task Force Spearhead / 3-227 Assault Aviation at Shindand Airfield supporting western Afghanistan;
- 2011 direct sources document the new Shindand rotary-wing staging/service apron and related fuel/munitions infrastructure;
- apron capacity is not aircraft inventory;
- Task Force Spearhead aggregate fuel throughput is not Shindand-only consumption.

Therefore no new Shindand AH-64/UH-60/CH-47 quantity is created from this magazine set.

# OMW design implications supported by this source set

The magazine evidence strengthens, but does not by itself make binding, the following historical design principles:

1. Army Aviation in Afghanistan routinely used split-based, mixed task-force structures rather than treating an entire CAB as one physical base inventory.
2. Large aviation hubs such as Bagram combined ramp, passenger/cargo, FARP, maintenance, parts, recovery, and Air Force/Army interface functions.
3. Forward support was distributed through FARPs, jump-FARP capability, maintenance contact teams, armament support, convoy support, and DART elements.
4. Nominal aircraft inventory must remain distinct from mission-available aircraft because maintenance, phase inspection, RESET, recovery, and redeployment consume real fleet capacity.
5. Dynamic retasking and hasty air assault were historically used, but any DCS/MOOSE implementation requires separate technical verification.
6. Terrain, altitude, line of sight, route predictability, and small-arms/RPG threats materially shaped rotary-wing employment.
7. Brigade-level flight-hour, mission, patient, cargo, and fuel figures are useful as top-down plausibility checks and must not be allocated to a single base without source support.
